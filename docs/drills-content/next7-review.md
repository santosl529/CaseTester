# Part 1. Drill descriptions

What the student reads on the drill card.

**EX-4.** Write the one takeaway from a chart that matters to the client, with the number that proves it.

*Why:* In the interview there are no options to choose from: you find the "so what" yourself, in seconds.

**EX-5.** Fix a flawed chart takeaway so it answers the client's question with a number the chart supports.

*Why:* A takeaway that's beside the point, or reads the chart wrong, costs trust fast; this trains you to catch it before you say it.

**CL-4.** Propose the next analysis and why, then keep or change it when a new fact arrives.

*Why:* Interviewers expect you to drive the case, and to change course when the data surprises you.

# Part 2. Lesson: CL.next_step v2

## The idea
After each finding, propose the next analysis yourself: the one that moves the case furthest toward the answer.

## Why interviewers care
Candidates who wait to be told what to do score lower on **case leadership** (driving the case forward yourself). A good next step shows you know where the answer probably is.

## Why it works
Each analysis should shrink the space where the answer can be. The step that tests the most likely cause, or the biggest open question, shrinks it most. Proposing it yourself shows you're leading the case, not following it.

## The pattern
1. Say what the latest finding means.
2. Pick the step that tests the most likely cause or the biggest open question.
3. Skip steps that are already answered or off the objective.
4. Don't jump to a solution before you know the cause.
5. Propose it: "Next, I'd like to look at…"
6. When a new fact arrives, decide: keep your step if the fact leaves you on the same driver, and go one level deeper; change it if the fact rules that driver out or points to a different one. Say why, using the fact.

## Worked example
Objective: find out why an online cookware shop's orders fell 25%.
Latest finding: site visits are flat.

"Visits are flat, so fewer visitors are ordering. **Next, I'd look at where visitors drop off between product page, cart and checkout.**"

Weaker choices: "Let's look at the number of visits" (already answered); "Let's cut prices" (a fix before the cause); "Let's review the supplier contracts" (sensible, but it matters less right now).

Then a new fact: checkout errors doubled after a site update. "**I'd keep my step**: the drop-off is likely at checkout, so I'd go a level deeper and find which errors stop orders."

## Another example
Objective: decide whether a planetarium should open a café.
Latest finding: 60% of visitors say they'd buy food on site.

"So there's demand. **Next, I'd estimate what the café would earn a year against what it costs to build and staff.**"

Weaker choices: "Let's survey visitors about food" (already answered); "Let's design the menu" (a detail before the decision); "Let's look at ticket prices" (off the objective).

## Tricky cases
- **A sensible step at the wrong time.** Checking supplier contracts isn't wrong, but when the finding points to checkout, it matters less right now.
- **A hypothesis to test isn't a fix.** "Next, I'd check whether checkout errors rose" tests a cause. "Fix the checkout" jumps to a solution.
- **Keeping is a decision too.** "Keep" needs a reason from the new fact, not "it still makes sense."
- **A fact that answers your step can still mean keep.** If it confirms your driver, keep it and go one level deeper: checkout errors doubled, so find which error stops orders. Change only when the fact rules your driver out or points to a different one.

## Common mistakes
- `M.waited_for_interviewer`: stopping after a finding without proposing what to do next.
- `M.illogical_next_step`: a step that's already answered, off the objective, or a fix before the cause is known.
- `M.low_priority_next_step`: a sensible analysis that matters less right now than another.

## Quick check
Objective: find out why a dry cleaner's profit fell 20%. Latest finding: revenue is flat. What's the next step?

**Answer:** Costs: revenue is flat, so the fall is in costs. "Next, I'd look at cost per garment by line (labor, chemicals, rent), this year against last."

### ex4-0001

*Tier 1 · public sector · Public / Education / Nonprofit · similarity: passed*

**Prompt**

> A city's library system lost about 28,000 visits this year, and the library board wants to know where to focus.
> 
> What's the takeaway from this chart that matters most for the client? Give the number that shows it.

**Chart: Visits by branch (thousands)** (grouped bar; Thousands of visits)

|  | Last year | This year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Central | 120 | 118 |
| Eastside | 80 | 79 |
| Riverside | 60 | 34 |
| Hillcrest | 40 | 41 |

**Key insight:** Riverside accounts for almost all the drop: its visits fell 26,000 (60,000 to 34,000) of the 28,000 lost, while the other branches held steady

**Chart figures:**
- Riverside fell 26k (60k to 34k), a 43% drop
- Total fell 28k (300k to 272k), about 9%
- Riverside's share of the drop is about 93% (26k of 28k)
- Central fell 2k (120k to 118k)
- Eastside fell 1k (80k to 79k)
- Hillcrest rose 1k (40k to 41k)

**Model answer**

> Riverside explains almost all the drop: its visits fell from 60,000 to 34,000, 26,000 of the 28,000 lost, while the other branches held steady, so the board should focus there.

**Follow-up (justify):** What does this mean for the client's decision?

**Model reply**

> Focus on Riverside: find out what changed there this year, such as hours, a closure or a new branch nearby, before spending on the whole system, since the other branches are steady.

**Explanation:** The takeaway that matters: Riverside accounts for almost all the drop: its visits fell 26,000 (60,000 to 34,000) of the 28,000 lost, while the other branches held steady. Figures: Riverside fell 26k (60k to 34k), a 43% drop; Total fell 28k (300k to 272k), about 9%; Riverside's share of the drop is about 93% (26k of 28k); Central fell 2k (120k to 118k); Eastside fell 1k (80k to 79k); Hillcrest rose 1k (40k to 41k).

**New fact (stage 2):** The snowmaking added 28 open days a season, 24 of them in November and early December, when the resort averages 300 skiers a day. On February weekends it averages 4,000, and lift lines stay under 10 minutes.

**Families (stage 1) and the right call for each:**

| Family | Covers | Right call | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Extra days in quiet weeks | The added days come when few people ski | Keep | 24 of the 28 added days come when 300 skiers a day show up |
| Busy days already full | No room for more skiers at peak | Drop | Lift lines stay under 10 minutes on February weekends |
| Too few added days | Snowmaking added too little season | Revise | It added 28 days; the problem is when they fall, not how many |

**Model answer**

> Stage 1: Visits likely rose only 4% because the extra snow days fall in weeks when few people ski anyway, such as early December. I'd compare visits by week with the days the snowmaking added.
> Stage 2: Keep: 24 of the 28 extra days fall in early season, when 300 skiers a day come, so the added days add few visits.

**Follow-up (justify):** What data would you ask for first to test your hypothesis, and what result would make you drop it?

**Model reply**

> Visits by week this season and the two before, marking the added days. If the added days drew as many skiers as an average day, I'd drop it.

**Explanation:** The new fact: The snowmaking added 28 open days a season, 24 of them in November and early December, when the resort averages 300 skiers a day. On February weekends it averages 4,000, and lift lines stay under 10 minutes. Extra days in quiet weeks: keep (24 of the 28 added days come when 300 skiers a day show up). Busy days already full: drop (Lift lines stay under 10 minutes on February weekends). Too few added days: revise (It added 28 days; the problem is when they fall, not how many).
